2.7×

The break

Glue the newer VIIRS satellite onto the old MODIS record and fire activity "jumps" 2.7× in 2012. The jump comes from the instrument, not from more fires.

2 Nov

The deadline

Suomi NPP stopped sending data on 2 Nov 2026. Terra and Aqua (MODIS) have drifted far off schedule and will shut down within a year or so.

~60

The competition

About 60 public team repos. We read the best 8 line by line. About half have no working demo, and most test themselves on data they already trained on.

What we will build

1
A harmonized record, 2000 to today. For every fire VIIRS saw, we estimate how MODIS would have recorded it. The estimate is learned from thousands of moments when both satellites passed at nearly the same time.
2
A burning calendar for any area you draw. A month × year grid, "this month vs normal", season dates, and a CSV download, all on a fast static website.
3
A trust check on every number. We compare our own correction error with normal year-to-year variation and say "reliable", "use with care" or "too uncertain to call".
4
Honest tests. Rules written before running; the model chosen on development data; a pass/fail check on regions we have never opened; a drift-proof satellite-vs-satellite test.
In plain English

Two generations of satellites count fires differently, like two thermometers that disagree. The old one is being switched off. We build a "translator" so the whole 25-year history reads as if one instrument measured it. Then we show it as a calendar fire chiefs can read, and we always say how sure we are.

1 · The problem

Why the fire record breaks

Shape based on our Portugal results (illustration, not to scale).

Three reasons the satellites disagree

  • Sharper eyes. VIIRS pixels are 375 m; MODIS pixels are 1 km and grow to about 5 km at the edge of its view. VIIRS sees many small fires MODIS cannot.
  • Different times of day. Fires follow a daily rhythm. Terra used to pass at 10:30 and Aqua at 13:30; by Feb 2026 they had drifted to about 09:00 and 15:15.
  • Satellites retire. Suomi NPP ended on 2 Nov 2026. Its replacements, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, need their own calibration.
In plain English

Imagine counting cars on a road for 12 years with a blurry camera, then switching to a sharp one. The new camera counts more cars, not because traffic grew but because it sees motorbikes the old one missed. If you join the two counts naively, you invent a traffic boom.

Our job: translate the sharp camera's counts into "blurry-camera units" so the whole 25 years compare fairly.

3 · Our angle

Four things the field doesn't have

① A learned detection response

For each fire VIIRS saw, how MODIS would have recorded it, depending on the fire's power. It is learned from matched passes of the two satellites. It's the harmonizer itself, not a side chart.

② A trust check on every month

Our correction error compared with the area's normal year-to-year variation: reliable use with care too uncertain.

③ A drift-proof test

We build the record twice, once from Suomi NPP and once from NOAA-20. If the two agree, the method works, and MODIS's drift can't fool the test.

④ A drift-divergence chart

From 2022 real MODIS drifts away from what it "should" see. We show this as a finding: what happened to NASA's longest fire record.

In plain English

4 · The algorithm

Four layers, from raw fires to a trusted calendar

In plain English

Layer 1: the new satellite often breaks one fire into several small dots, so we glue nearby dots back into one fire and add up its heat.

Layer 2: for each fire we ask "how likely was the old satellite to notice it?" A huge fire: almost certainly. A tiny one: rarely. We learn those odds from days when both satellites flew over the same fires.

Layer 3: a statistics step fixes what the odds miss: seasons, regions, and small fires the new satellite didn't catch.

Layer 4: we rerun everything hundreds of times with slightly different data to get an honest "give or take", and we check that the ranges actually contain the truth as often as they claim.

4 · The algorithm · the core idea

The detection response: the heart of the method

First measurement, before the chance-match correction. The rate for the smallest fires may partly be coincidence; we subtract that using a "±1 day" control.

Why learn it, rather than copy it from a paper

A 2026 paper (Sofiev & Kouznetsov, GMD) gives detection curves from physics. On our data they predicted Aqua's night counts within 1%, but for the smallest fires they say about 0.2% while matched data say about 13%. So we use the paper as a cross-check and learn the curve from the satellites themselves.

Why "VIIRS → MODIS" and not the other way round

Turning a sharp picture into a blurry one is always possible. Turning a blurry picture sharp means inventing detail, here inventing small fires MODIS never saw. Most competitors go the other way.

In plain English

A 1 MW fire is like a candle: the old satellite almost never notices it. A 100 MW fire is a bonfire: it usually does. We measured these odds ourselves by finding moments when both satellites flew over the same fires within an hour. Then, for every fire the new satellite sees today, we can say "the old satellite would have counted this as 0.03 of a fire" or "as 0.8 of a fire", and add those up.

4 · The algorithm · the calendar

From record to calendar, and how sure we are

What each calendar cell shows

  • Count: MODIS-equivalent hotspots that month (the middle of 300 draws).
  • Normal: the median and the mean of that month over 2003–last year. Bridged 2000–02 years are flagged, not used as "normal".
  • Anomaly: % above or below the multi-year mean (as the challenge asks), plus the month's rank, e.g. "85th percentile".
  • Colour: cividis for counts, a purple–orange diverging ramp for anomalies (both safe for colour-blind readers); uncertain cells fade toward grey.
  • Season: start, middle and end dates (10/50/90% of the season's fires), only where there are at least 20 fires.

The trust check

Plus "Few fires here" when a typical month has fewer than 5 detections.

In plain English

Some places naturally swing a lot from year to year: one August has 10 fires, the next has 200. If our correction is "give or take 5", it barely matters there. In a quiet place where every August has 3 or 4 fires, "give or take 5" swamps everything. The trust check says which situation you are in, so nobody panics over a "red" month that is really just noise.

Before 2003 and after Suomi NPP

  • 2000 to mid-2002: only Terra flew, so the record would show a 2× step in 2002. We bridge it using Terra's typical share and mark those months "bridged".
  • After 2 Nov 2026: NOAA-20 gets its own learned curve. NOAA-21 is translated via NOAA-20, because it never flew alongside an on-schedule MODIS. Available VIIRS satellites are averaged, as the GFED5 fire dataset does.

How we test it, honestly

TestWhat it provesPass rule
Accuracy 2020–21We can rebuild MODIS from VIIRS on years and places we never trained onBetter score than the baseline; 80% ranges cover 70–90% of the time
Satellite swapThe method doesn't depend on which VIIRS satellite you feed it, and it is immune to MODIS driftRecords from Suomi NPP and NOAA-20 agree in at least 80% of months
2012 seamThe false jump is goneReported as a number with a range, not claimed "proven"
Drift divergence 2022–25Shows when MODIS itself went wrongA result, not a score
In plain English

It's like a student who mustn't see the exam. We practise on some years and places. Then we write the method down and lock it. Only then do we open regions we have never looked at (Morocco, Tunisia, central Chile, South Africa's Western Cape) and take the exam once. Pass or fail, we publish the grade. Most teams marked their own homework; judges notice.

Scores used: CRPS (a fair score for a forecast with a range), coverage (do 80% ranges contain the truth 80% of the time?), WAPE (average % error) and season-date error in days.

6 · The product

What a judge clicks in the first 30 seconds

MVP features (must exist on 15 Nov)

  • Static site (no server), cold load under 5 s
  • Draw an area, type lat/lon, or pick a country
  • Month × year calendar, 2000 → 2026 (2026 marked provisional)
  • "Naive vs harmonized" toggle: watch the 2012 jump disappear
  • This month vs median and mean, rank, trust check
  • Season dates, drift-divergence chart
  • Two CSVs: raw hotspots with weights; monthly with ranges
  • Keyboard-navigable calendar with a table fallback
In plain English

Open the link and you immediately see a fire calendar: rows are years, columns are months, brighter means more fire. Draw any shape on the map and the calendar updates for that area. A badge tells you whether to trust it. Two buttons download the data. Nothing to install, nothing to log in to, and it works on a phone.

9 · Reference

Glossary and reading list

MODIS
The older fire-detecting instrument, on the Terra (2000) and Aqua (2002) satellites. 1 km pixels. Retiring.
VIIRS
The newer instrument, on Suomi NPP (2012–2026), NOAA-20 (2018) and NOAA-21 (2024). 375 m pixels: it sees smaller fires.
Hotspot / detection
One satellite pixel flagged as burning. Not the same as a fire (one fire can make many) or as burned area.
FIRMS
NASA's fire data service, where all our hotspot data comes from.
FRP (fire radiative power)
How much heat a fire gives off, in megawatts (MW). Big fires have high FRP.
Harmonize
Make two records comparable, as if one instrument measured both.
Overpass / drift
The time a satellite flies over. Terra and Aqua's times have drifted, so they now see fires at different hours than they used to.
Negative binomial
A statistical model for counts that can swing a lot (like fires).
Bootstrap
Re-running the analysis many times on reshuffled data to measure uncertainty.
CRPS / coverage
CRPS scores a forecast that gives a range. Coverage checks whether "80% ranges" really contain the truth 80% of the time.
Leakage
Accidentally letting the test data influence the model, which makes results look better than they are.
Sealed region
A region we promise not to look at until the model is frozen, so the final test is fair.
